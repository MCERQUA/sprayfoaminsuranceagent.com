# Workers' Comp Class Codes for Spray Foam Crews: Getting Them Right

Workers' compensation premium is driven almost entirely by two things: payroll, and the class code assigned to that payroll. For most trades, the classification is straightforward — a roofer is a roofer, a plumber is a plumber. Spray foam sits in a murkier spot, and getting the classification wrong is one of the most common, and most expensive, mistakes spray foam contractors make on their workers' comp program.

## Why Spray Foam Doesn't Fit a "Default" Classification Cleanly

Insulation work as a broad category covers a wide range of actual labor — from stapling fiberglass batts between studs to spraying a two-component chemical foam under pressure with a heated hose and proportioning rig. Rating bureaus and carriers don't always default to the same classification for both, and the difference matters because the underlying hazard profile is genuinely different:

- **Chemical exposure** — spray foam involves handling and applying isocyanate-based chemicals, which is a materially different hazard than handling fiberglass or cellulose
- **Respiratory and PPE requirements** — proper spray foam application requires supplied-air or cartridge respirators, protective suits, and controlled ventilation, none of which apply to standard insulation installation
- **Equipment operation** — proportioners, heated hose systems, and spray rigs introduce equipment-related injury exposure that a hand-tool insulation job doesn't carry
- **Working conditions** — attic, crawlspace, and enclosed-space work is common in both trades, but the combination of confined space and airborne chemical exposure is specific to spray application

A classification that was written with batt or blown-in insulation in mind doesn't reflect any of that. Depending on your state's rating bureau, there may be a more specific classification available for spray application work, or the correct approach may involve documentation and underwriting conversation rather than a single "correct" code pulled from a manual. Class code structures vary by state and by whether the state uses NCCI or an independent rating bureau, which is exactly why this isn't a one-size-fits-all answer — it's worth confirming directly for your state rather than assuming your neighbor's classification applies to you.

## The Two Ways Misclassification Costs You

**Overpaying.** If your crew's actual work is being lumped into a higher-hazard general contracting or roofing classification because nobody separated out the insulation-specific labor, you may be paying comp premium calculated against a hazard class that doesn't reflect what your crew actually does most of the time.

**Undercoverage and audit exposure.** The more common and more dangerous mistake runs the other way — labor gets classified under a lower-hazard code that doesn't actually describe spray application work. This can look fine at binding, but it creates real exposure at two points: a workers' comp audit that reclassifies payroll after the fact (creating a surprise additional premium bill), and — more seriously — a claim scenario where the carrier's investigation reveals the actual work performed doesn't match the classification on file.

## Payroll Documentation Matters as Much as the Code Itself

Even with the right classification identified, how you document and separate payroll matters. If your crew splits time between spray application and other work — trim carpentry, general contracting, standard insulation — properly separated payroll records let your policy reflect the actual mix of hazard exposure, rather than defaulting your entire crew into whichever single classification is easiest to apply. Carriers that understand spray foam operations are generally more willing to work with properly documented payroll splits than carriers underwriting the account as a generic insulation or general contracting risk.

## What to Do Before Your Next Audit or Renewal

1. **Ask your current agent or carrier directly what classification your spray foam labor is coded under**, and whether that classification exists specifically for spray application in your state, or whether it's a general insulation or contracting code being applied by default.
2. **Separate and document payroll** by task type if your crew does more than spray application, so your policy — and your premium — reflect actual exposure.
3. **Get a second opinion from an agent who places spray foam accounts regularly.** Class code selection isn't something every general commercial agent handles the same way, and it's worth confirming rather than assuming your current setup is correct.

Workers' comp classification isn't the most exciting part of running a spray foam operation, but it's one of the few places where getting it wrong shows up twice — once in your premium, and again, at the worst possible time, in a claim.
